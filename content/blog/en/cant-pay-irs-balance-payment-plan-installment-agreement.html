If you received a tax bill, or your return shows tax due that you cannot pay all at once, the IRS lets you apply for a payment plan (installment agreement). Eligibility and fees depend on the amount you owe and how you apply. Below is a summary of the IRS page.

IRS Announcement

“Interest and some penalty charges continue to be added to the amount you owe until the balance is paid in full.”IRS, Payment plans; installment agreements · Read the original

Key Points

  • Long-term payment plan: Individuals can apply online if they owe $50,000 or less in combined tax, penalties and interest and have filed all required returns.
  • Short-term payment plan (180 days or less): Available if you owe less than $100,000 in combined tax, penalties and interest. Only individuals can apply online.
  • Businesses: Call the number on your notice or 800-829-4933, or visit a Taxpayer Assistance Center. Sole proprietors and independent contractors apply as individuals.
  • Setup fees (online / phone, mail or in person): Pay in full $0 / $0; short-term plan $0 / $0; long-term plan with Direct Debit $29 / $107; long-term plan with other payment methods $69 / $178. Changing an existing non-Direct Debit plan is $6 / $89.
  • Low-income reduction: If adjusted gross income is at or below 250% of the applicable federal poverty level, fees may be reduced, waived or reimbursed (for long-term plans entered on or after April 10, 2018).
  • Interest and penalties: They keep accruing on a payment plan until the balance is paid in full, including on a short-term plan.
  • How to apply: Through your IRS online account, or by mailing Form 9465 or calling (individuals: 800-829-1040).
  • Avoiding default: Pay at least the minimum monthly amount when due, file all required returns on time, and pay later taxes in full and on time. Future refunds are applied to your tax debt until it is paid in full.
  • Collection: The IRS says it generally does not take enforced collection action while a plan is being considered or is in effect, for 30 days after a request is rejected or a plan is terminated, or while an appeal is evaluated.

Eunsang Lee, EA: What This Means for You

  • First check that every required return has been filed. A missing year can block the online application for a long-term plan, so even if you think you cannot pay, I recommend filing first (my opinion, not stated on the IRS page).
  • A payment plan lets you pay over time. It does not reduce what you owe. Interest and some penalties keep accruing, so it may be worth considering paying ahead when you can (my opinion, not stated on the IRS page). The exact amounts need individual confirmation.
  • Fees vary widely by how you apply. For the same long-term plan, applying online with Direct Debit carries a lower fee under the IRS page. This is general advice, not from the IRS page: to avoid default you must pay at least the minimum monthly amount when due, so choose an amount you can realistically pay.
  • The most common problem after a plan starts is missing new taxes or a required filing. Review your future estimated payments or withholding at the same time.
  • Whether a payment plan is right, or whether penalty relief or another option fits better, depends on your situation.
