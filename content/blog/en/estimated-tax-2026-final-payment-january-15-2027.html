If you have income that is not subject to withholding, the IRS says to pay estimated tax in installments during the year. The fourth and final payment for 2026 income is due January 15, 2027. This summary follows the IRS estimated taxes page (last reviewed or updated September 25, 2026) and the 2026 Form 1040-ES package.

IRS Announcement

“You don't have to make the payment due January 15, 2027, if you file your 2026 tax return by February 1, 2027, and pay the entire balance due with your return.”IRS, 2026 Form 1040-ES · Read the original

Key Points

  • Who must pay: The Form 1040-ES package says that in most cases you must pay estimated tax for 2026 if both of the following apply. (1) You expect to owe at least $1,000 in tax for 2026, after subtracting your withholding and refundable credits. (2) You expect your withholding and refundable credits to be less than the smaller of 90% of the tax to be shown on your 2026 tax return, or 100% of the tax shown on your 2025 tax return (your 2025 return must cover all 12 months).
  • The 110% rule: If your adjusted gross income (AGI) for 2025 was more than $150,000 ($75,000 if married filing separately for 2026), substitute 110% for 100% in the 2025 prior-year test.
  • Due dates: You can pay all of your estimated tax by April 15, 2026, or in four equal amounts by April 15, 2026, June 15, 2026, September 15, 2026 and January 15, 2027. If a due date falls on a weekend or legal holiday, a payment made the next business day is on time.
  • Skipping the January 15 payment: You do not have to make the payment due January 15, 2027, if you file your 2026 return by February 1, 2027, and pay the entire balance due with your return.
  • Who does not have to pay: You do not have to pay estimated tax for 2026 if you were a U.S. citizen or resident alien for all of 2025 and you had no tax liability for the full 12-month 2025 tax year. You also do not need to pay if you expect to owe less than $1,000 after withholding and refundable credits.
  • Wage earners: You can avoid estimated tax by having your employer withhold more, using a new Form W-4.
  • How to pay: your IRS online account (IRS.gov/Account), IRS Direct Pay, debit or credit card (a provider fee applies), electronic funds withdrawal when e-filing, EFTPS (enrollment required), the IRS2Go app, phone, cash at approved locations, or a check or money order mailed with the payment voucher. Do not send cash through the mail.
  • Penalty: If you do not pay enough through withholding and estimated payments, you may have to pay a penalty for underpayment of estimated tax. It may be waived for a casualty, disaster or unusual circumstance, or if you retired after age 62 or became disabled. Form 2210 determines whether you owe it.

Eunsang Lee, EA: What This Means for You

  • People most likely to be affected are those with freelance or 1099 (independent contractor) income, a business, rental income, or interest, dividends and investment gains, where withholding is not enough. If you only have wages, this usually does not apply, but check if you also have side income.
  • Even if you already made some 2026 estimated payments, it is worth recalculating your income before the final due date. Your income may have changed a lot late in the year. How much you need to pay depends on your income, deductions and withholding, so it needs to be calculated individually.
  • This is my own opinion, not IRS guidance: to skip the January 15 payment by filing by February 1, you need your W-2 and 1099 documents in hand by early January. If documents arrive late, this approach may not be practical, so check the timing first.
  • Whether the 100% or the 110% test applies depends on whether your 2025 AGI was above $150,000 and whether your 2025 return covered 12 months. Which one applies to you needs to be checked individually.
  • The IRS says requirements differ for farmers, fishermen and certain higher-income taxpayers (see Publication 505), so if that applies to you, it needs to be checked individually.
  • This IRS guidance is about federal tax. California has its own estimated tax rules through the FTB, so state tax needs to be checked individually.
